# Commercial Insurance Underwriter Submission Narrative

## Signature Skill

**Insurance AI Advantage System — Signature Skill #1**

This is a fully engineered Apeironix Signature Skill designed to operate as part of the Insurance AI Advantage System.

Unlike a single-purpose prompt, this Skill combines:

- Exposure interpretation
- Underwriting reasoning
- Submission readiness
- Evidence classification
- Risk positioning
- Loss analysis
- Concern anticipation
- Data-gap detection
- Narrative generation
- Human review
- Downstream workflow handoff

The objective is not merely to write a better submission.

The objective is to create an underwriting-ready risk narrative that improves the efficiency and quality of the entire placement workflow.

---

# Purpose

Transform raw commercial insurance intake information into a structured, credible, decision-ready submission narrative that an underwriter can understand and evaluate quickly.

This Skill is designed to help the insurance professional:

- Convert fragmented intake data into an organized underwriting story
- Explain how the business actually operates
- Identify the exposures that materially affect underwriting
- Present loss history in useful context
- Surface meaningful risk controls
- Highlight favorable characteristics without overselling the account
- Anticipate likely underwriting concerns
- Address concerns proactively where evidence supports doing so
- Identify missing information before the submission reaches the market
- Reduce unnecessary underwriting follow-up
- Improve submission readability
- Improve underwriting confidence
- Improve quote velocity
- Improve the probability of obtaining meaningful terms

The objective is **not** to make every account appear attractive.

The objective is to make every submission:

> Clear, credible, complete, and easy for an underwriter to evaluate.

---

# Core Outcome

A successful execution should allow an underwriter to understand the account in approximately two minutes and answer:

1. What does this company do?
2. How large is it?
3. Where does it operate?
4. What creates the primary loss potential?
5. What does the loss history tell me?
6. What controls are in place?
7. What makes the account potentially attractive?
8. What concerns should I evaluate?
9. What coverage is being requested?
10. What information still needs clarification?

The finished narrative should reduce the amount of investigative work required before underwriting can decide whether to:

- Proceed
- Request additional information
- Provide an indication
- Quote
- Refer internally
- Decline

# Document Completeness Standard

Classify the source package as:

### Complete Enough for Submission Narrative

Enough information exists to produce a credible underwriting narrative.

### Partially Complete

Narrative can be drafted, but meaningful information remains outstanding.

### Fragmentary

Insufficient information exists to responsibly position the account.

Do not hide incomplete source material behind polished writing.

---

# Source Hierarchy

When information conflicts:

1. Current client-confirmed information
2. Current authoritative source document
3. Current application
4. Current schedule

8. Historical data
9. Professional inference

Identify material conflicts rather than silently choosing one version.

# Evidence Classification

Classify material statements internally as:

## Verified Account Fact

Supported by client confirmation or authoritative documents.

## Documented Loss Fact

Supported by current loss information.

## Documented Risk Control

Supported by evidence that the control exists.

## Broker Observation

Professional observation supported by available information.

## Reasonable Underwriting Inference

A logical conclusion based on known operations.

## Assumption

Information used provisionally because a detail is missing.

## Requires Confirmation

Material information requiring verification.

Never convert inference into client fact.

---

# Authority vs. Inference Standard

Distinguish clearly between:

### Client Fact

What the insured confirms.

### Source Fact

What applications, schedules, policies, or loss runs demonstrate.

### AI / Broker Analysis

Interpretation of the exposure.

### Underwriting Decision

The carrier's responsibility.

The narrative should help underwriting evaluate the account.

It should never imply that the broker determines carrier appetite.

---

# Neutral Narrative / Attribution Standard

Where information involves:

- Loss cause
- Fault
- Claim circumstances
- Disputed facts
- Allegations
- Corrective actions

preserve attribution.

Use:

> The insured reports that corrective driver training was implemented following the loss.

not:

> The loss was fully corrected through driver training.

Use:

> Loss runs reflect three auto claims during the period reviewed.

not:

> The insured has an auto frequency problem.

The latter may be an underwriting interpretation and should be labeled accordingly.

# Core Principle

## Make the Risk Easy to Understand Without Making It Look Better Than It Is

A strong submission narrative reduces underwriting friction through:

- Clarity
- Context
- Organization
- Credibility
- Anticipation

not through:

- Marketing language
- Minimization
- Selective omission
- Unsupported optimism

The narrative should make the underwriter think:

> I understand this account, I understand the major exposures, and I know what I need to evaluate next.

---

# Underwriter Two-Minute Standard

The core narrative should allow an experienced underwriter to understand the risk in approximately two minutes.

Prioritize:

1. Operations
2. Size
3. Geography
4. Material exposures
5. Loss experience
6. Controls
7. Underwriting strengths
8. Significant concerns

Secondary operational detail should not overwhelm the primary story.

---

# Execution Readiness Standard

Before finalizing the narrative, classify:

### Submission Ready

Enough material underwriting information exists to support market submission.

### Conditionally Ready

Narrative is usable, but material items should accompany or quickly follow the submission.

### Not Submission Ready

Critical information is missing or contradictory.

Do not equate:

> Narrative completed

with:

> Account ready for market.

---

# Workflow

## Step 1 — Establish the Business Model

Determine:

- What the company sells
- What services it performs
- Where revenue is generated
- Who performs the work
- Where work occurs
- Who the customers are
- Whether products, installation, manufacturing, transportation, professional services, or subcontracting are involved

Do not rely solely on the company name or industry label.

---

## Step 2 — Establish Account Scale

Capture relevant measures:

- Revenue
- Payroll
- Employees
- Locations
- Fleet
- Property values
- Geographic footprint

Use only documented values.

---

## Step 3 — Identify Primary Operations

Describe what creates the majority of business activity and insurance exposure.

Avoid cataloging every minor task.

---

## Step 4 — Identify Secondary Operations

Determine whether secondary activities materially change underwriting.

Examples:

- Installation
- Delivery
- Subcontracting
- Residential work
- International activity
- Professional services
- Manufacturing
- Hazardous work

Include only material activities.

---

## Step 5 — Develop the Exposure Architecture

Identify major exposures across relevant lines.

Potential areas:

### General Liability

- Premises
- Operations
- Products
- Completed operations
- Contractual risk
- Subcontractors

### Commercial Auto

- Fleet size
- Vehicle type
- Radius
- Driver population
- Hired / non-owned exposure

### Workers' Compensation

- Employee duties
- Payroll concentration
- Field work
- Heavy labor
- Height
- Machinery

### Property

- Construction
- Occupancy
- Protection
- Values
- CAT exposure
- Business interruption

### Professional / Specialty

- E&O
- Cyber
- Pollution
- Management liability
- Other specialty exposure

Do not force every line into the narrative.

---

## Step 6 — Identify Frequency vs. Severity Drivers

Determine which exposures are more likely to create:

### Frequency

Repeated smaller claims.

### Severity

Lower-frequency but potentially significant losses.

This helps underwriting understand where attention belongs.

---

## Step 7 — Analyze Loss Experience

Review:

- Number of losses
- Total incurred
- Open vs. closed
- Frequency
- Severity
- Large claims
- Recurrence
- Recent deterioration
- Improvement

Do not summarize loss runs without interpretation.

---

# Loss Narrative Standard

For meaningful losses, address when supported:

1. What happened
2. Approximate financial impact
3. Whether the issue appears isolated or recurring
4. Corrective action taken
5. Whether similar losses occurred afterward
6. What remains unresolved

Do not minimize losses.

Do not state corrective actions occurred unless documented.

---

## Step 8 — Identify Risk Controls

Capture controls that actually affect the exposure.

Examples:

- Safety program
- Fleet telematics
- MVR review
- Driver training
- Contract review
- Certificate tracking
- Quality control
- Equipment maintenance
- Cyber controls
- Employee training
- Property protection
- Claims review process

Avoid generic language such as:

> The insured takes safety seriously.

Prefer evidence.

---

# Control Verification Standard

Classify risk controls as:

### Verified Control

Supported by documentation or client confirmation.

### Reported Control

Client states the control exists but no supporting evidence is available.

### Proposed Control

Planned but not yet implemented.

Do not present proposed controls as existing.

---

## Step 9 — Assess Management Quality

Where information supports it, identify:

- Years in business
- Management experience
- Ownership continuity
- Operational stability
- Safety leadership
- Claims management
- Growth discipline

Avoid subjective praise unsupported by evidence.

---

## Step 10 — Identify Underwriting Strengths

Potential strengths include:

- Long operating history
- Stable management
- Strong loss performance
- Improvement in losses
- Formal safety controls
- Diversified customers
- Controlled growth
- Low CAT exposure
- Strong contractual controls
- Newer fleet
- Telematics
- Experienced workforce

Select only those relevant to the account.

---

## Step 11 — Identify Underwriting Concerns

Potential concerns include:

- Loss frequency
- Large claim
- Rapid growth
- Fleet exposure
- Residential work
- Hazardous operations
- High employee turnover
- CAT concentration
- Weak contract controls
- Limited loss history
- New operations
- Unclear payroll allocation
- Missing values
- High subcontractor usage

Do not hide concerns.

---

## Step 12 — Develop Proactive Concern Responses

For each major concern, ask:

1. What will the underwriter notice?
2. What question will likely follow?
3. Do we already have the answer?
4. What control or context matters?
5. What evidence supports the response?

Example:

### Concern

Three preventable auto claims in two years.

### Useful Positioning

Describe:

- What occurred
- Whether the drivers remain employed
- Telematics implementation
- MVR standards
- Driver training
- Subsequent loss experience

when supported.

---

# Concern Response Standard

Address concerns with:

**Fact → Context → Action → Current State**

Do not use:

**Concern → Excuse**

---

## Step 13 — Identify Coverage Requested

Clearly state:

- Lines
- Limits
- Deductible / retention where relevant
- Special coverage requirements
- Umbrella / excess structure

Do not invent terms that were not requested.

---

## Step 14 — Determine Submission Differentiators

Ask:

> Why might an underwriter prefer spending time on this submission?

Potential differentiators:

- Complete information
- Strong controls
- Favorable loss trend
- Professional management
- Good documentation
- Clear risk narrative
- Realistic coverage request
- Quality broker preparation

Do not use exaggerated promotional claims.

---

## Step 15 — Identify Missing Information

Classify missing information:

### Critical Before Submission

Likely prevents meaningful underwriting.

### Important Follow-Up

Submission may proceed, but underwriting will probably request it.

### Supplemental

Helpful but not material to initial review.

---

## Step 16 — Determine Submission Readiness

Classify:

- Submission Ready
- Conditionally Ready
- Not Submission Ready

Explain why.

---

## Step 17 — Write the Executive Summary

The Executive Summary should generally answer:

- Who is the company?
- What do they do?
- How large are they?
- Where do they operate?
- What is the overall underwriting perspective?

Keep it concise.

---

## Step 18 — Write the Operations Overview

Describe:

- Core operations
- Customers
- Work performed
- Geography
- Material secondary activities

Use underwriting language, not marketing language.

---

## Step 19 — Write the Risk Profile

Explain the primary exposure drivers.

Focus on:

- Frequency
- Severity
- Concentration
- Operational complexity

---

## Step 20 — Write the Loss Experience

Explain what the loss history means.

Do not merely recite numbers.

---

## Step 21 — Write Risk Controls & Mitigation

Connect controls directly to exposure.

Example:

Weak:

> The company has a strong safety culture.

Better:

> The company reviews MVRs annually, uses telematics across the fleet, and conducts quarterly driver coaching based on exception reports.

---

## Step 22 — Write Underwriting Positioning

Explain why the account merits consideration.

Potential factors:

- Risk quality
- Management
- Controls
- Loss improvement
- Stability
- Submission quality
- Opportunity for broader relationship

Maintain credibility.

---

## Step 23 — Write Notable Considerations

Include:

- Assumptions
- Data gaps
- Special circumstances
- Known underwriting issues
- Pending documentation
- Items requiring confirmation

This section prevents uncertainty from disappearing inside polished prose.

# Underwriting Readability Standard

The submission narrative should:

- Use clear headings
- Use short paragraphs
- Prioritize material facts
- Avoid unnecessary repetition
- Avoid excessive adjectives
- Avoid promotional language
- Avoid dumping raw schedules into narrative
- Avoid unexplained acronyms
- Avoid generic AI phrasing

The underwriter should be able to scan the submission quickly.

---

# Data Integrity Standard

Before drafting, review for:

- Conflicting revenue
- Conflicting payroll
- Different employee counts
- Different locations
- Missing values
- Duplicate losses
- Incorrect totals
- Stale exposure data
- Conflicting policy periods

Do not silently reconcile material inconsistencies.

---

# Calculation Integrity Standard

Where calculations are used:

- Use documented values
- Identify denominator
- Identify period
- Avoid false precision

Potential calculations:

- Loss frequency
- Loss severity
- Revenue concentration
- Payroll concentration
- Fleet claim frequency
- Residential percentage

Do not calculate ratios from incomplete data without disclosure.

---

# Change / Drift Detection Standard

Determine whether prior underwriting information may be stale because the business changed.

Potential changes:

- New operation
- Acquisition
- New state
- Rapid growth
- New fleet
- New product
- New subcontracting
- Changed customer mix
- New management
- New risk control

Do not recycle last year's narrative without revalidation.

---

# Comparison Basis / Like-for-Like Standard

Where prior-year data is referenced, confirm comparable:

- Period
- Revenue
- Payroll
- Operations
- Locations
- Loss periods
- Exposure basis

Avoid misleading trend statements.

# Dynamic Reassessment / Monitoring Trigger Standard

Reassess the narrative when:

- New loss information arrives
- Revenue changes materially
- Payroll changes materially
- New location is added
- New operation is disclosed
- Coverage request changes
- New carrier concern arises
- Client corrects information
- Risk controls change
- Submission becomes stale

Do not treat the original narrative as permanently accurate.

---

# Stop Rules

Stop narrative finalization and escalate when:

- Business operations cannot be reliably understood
- Material exposure data conflicts
- Significant losses are unexplained
- Required coverage is unclear
- Client information appears unreliable
- Material information is missing
- Market authorization is unclear
- Submission would contain unsupported representations

Do not solve poor intake with better writing.

---

# Completion Criteria Standard

The narrative workflow is complete when:

1. Core operations are understood
2. Account scale is documented
3. Material exposures are identified
4. Loss history is interpreted
5. Controls are documented accurately
6. Underwriting strengths are identified
7. Material concerns are addressed
8. Coverage request is clear
9. Assumptions are disclosed
10. Data gaps are identified
11. Readiness is classified
12. Narrative passes quality control
13. Human reviewer approves where required
14. Supporting submission package is identified

Narrative completion does not equal carrier submission unless the workflow includes authorized submission.

---

# Human Review Escalation Standard

Require heightened human review when:

- Serious loss exists
- Fatality exists
- Large unresolved claim exists
- Material coverage issue exists
- Rapidly changing operations exist
- High-hazard activity exists
- Information materially conflicts
- Submission contains significant assumptions
- Client wants unfavorable information omitted
- Market blocking risk exists
- Submission could create significant E&O exposure

AI may prepare the work.

Authorized marketing and insurance professionals approve the final submission.

---

- Internal sales strategy
- Agency revenue

- Market blocking strategy
- Negotiation leverage
- E&O concerns

- Unverified speculation

# Decision Rules

## Underwriting Relevance Over Completeness

Include what helps the underwriter evaluate the risk.

---

## Facts Before Positioning

The underwriting story must be based on real exposure.

---

## Strengths Must Be Supportable

Avoid generic praise.

---

## Concerns Should Be Addressed, Not Hidden

Proactive context improves credibility.

---

## Losses Need Context

Do not bury or minimize them.

---

## Controls Need Evidence

---

## Submission Quality Cannot Fix Poor Market Fit

A strong narrative does not create appetite where none exists.

---

## Assumptions Must Remain Visible

Never bury assumptions inside confident prose.

# Materiality / Prioritization Framework

| Factor | Question |
|---|---|

The Skill should not be considered complete if:
