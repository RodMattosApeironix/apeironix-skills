# Commercial Insurance Executive Proposal Summary

## Purpose

Transform complex commercial insurance information into a clear, executive-level narrative that helps a business owner or senior decision-maker understand the client’s current situation, material risks, recommended program, rationale, and expected outcomes.

This Skill is designed to help the insurance professional:

- Translate technical insurance details into business language
- Explain what matters without overwhelming the client
- Connect coverage recommendations to actual business risk
- Explain why a particular program structure was selected
- Clarify meaningful trade-offs between options
- Reinforce the value of the recommended approach
- Help executives make informed insurance decisions
- Create a proposal narrative that supports the producer’s recommendation

The objective is **not** to restate quote specifications.

The objective is to create a compelling, accurate, and decision-ready **Executive Proposal Summary**.

---

# Core Outcome

A successful use of this Skill should help the client understand:

- Where the business stands today
- Which risks matter most
- What the proposed program is designed to address
- Why the recommended option was selected
- What important trade-offs exist
- What the client gains from the proposed structure
- What risks remain
- What decisions are required
- What should happen next

The client should be able to understand the recommendation without needing to interpret technical insurance language.

- Use the most authoritative current source

# Evidence Classification

Every material proposal statement should be treated as one of the following.

## Documented Program Fact

Directly supported by the current program or quote.

Example:

> The recommended option provides a $5 million umbrella limit.

---

## Analytical Observation

A conclusion derived from comparing the program to the client’s known exposures.

Example:

> The larger umbrella limit provides more protection against catastrophic auto and liability claims.

---

## Recommendation

A professional judgment about which option best aligns with the client’s needs.

Example:

> We recommend Option B because it improves catastrophic liability protection while keeping the premium increase within the client’s stated tolerance.

---

## Assumption

A necessary but unverified assumption.

Example:

> This recommendation assumes the client’s contractual requirements do not exceed the proposed umbrella limits.

Assumptions must be labeled.

---

# Confidence Classification

Where useful, classify conclusions:

### High Confidence

Supported by direct quote, policy, or client information.

### Moderate Confidence

Supported by the available information but dependent on one or more unresolved details.

### Low Confidence

Requires meaningful additional information before recommendation.

Do not use false precision.

---

# Core Principle

## Explain the Business Decision, Not Just the Insurance

Executives do not need a list of coverage specifications without context.

The summary should explain:

- What the risk is
- Why it matters
- What is being recommended
- Why that approach is appropriate
- What trade-off is being made
- What outcome the client should expect

Translate insurance into business consequences.

---

# Workflow

Follow the workflow in order unless the circumstances clearly require another sequence.

---

## Step 1 — Establish the Client Context

Identify:

- What the company does
- Current insurance position
- Current carrier
- Current premium
- Current program structure
- Known problems
- Recent losses
- Business changes
- Client priorities
- Renewal pressures
- Contractual considerations
- Growth plans

The proposal narrative must reflect the client’s actual situation.

---

## Step 2 — Identify the Decision the Client Must Make

Determine the real decision.

Examples:

- Stay with incumbent or move
- Pay more for broader coverage
- Accept higher deductible for cost relief
- Increase umbrella limits
- Add cyber coverage
- Restructure property program
- Consolidate carriers
- Move to alternative risk financing
- Trade short-term premium for long-term stability

The narrative should center on the decision.

---

## Step 3 — Establish the Current Situation

Summarize the client’s present position.

Address when relevant:

- Current cost
- Current coverage
- Current limitations
- Recent losses
- Exposure growth
- Business changes
- Market pressure
- Carrier behavior
- Service issues
- Known gaps

Avoid turning this into a long historical recap.

Focus on why the decision is being made now.

---

## Step 4 — Identify the Material Risks

Identify only the risks that are central to the recommendation.

Examples:

- Catastrophic auto
- Property loss
- Business interruption
- Products liability
- Contractual liability
- Cyber loss
- Employment practices
- Professional liability
- Workers’ compensation
- Pollution
- Large liability loss
- Supply-chain interruption

For each major risk, translate it into plain language.

Instead of:

> Products/completed operations exposure is material.

Prefer:

> Because the company’s work remains in place after the job is completed, a defect could create liability long after the project is finished.

---

## Step 5 — Identify the Most Important Program Changes

Compare the proposed program to the client’s current position.

Identify material changes such as:

- Carrier
- Premium
- Limits
- Deductibles
- Retentions
- Coverage additions
- Coverage reductions
- Exclusions
- Sublimits
- Umbrella structure
- Property values
- Business-income treatment
- Cyber protection
- Executive-risk coverage
- Claims structure
- Service model

Do not list every change.

Prioritize changes that affect the client’s decision.

---

## Step 6 — Evaluate the Available Options

When multiple options exist, compare them based on:

- Cost
- Coverage breadth
- Limits
- Deductibles
- Retained risk
- Carrier quality
- Claims support
- Program stability
- Contract compliance
- Ease of administration
- Long-term fit

Do not recommend solely based on lowest premium.

---

## Step 7 — Identify the Recommended Program

State the recommendation clearly.

Example:

> We recommend Option B with the $5 million umbrella and $25,000 property deductible.

Do not bury the recommendation.

If the recommendation is conditional, say so.

Example:

> Subject to confirmation of the professional liability endorsement, Option B appears to provide the strongest overall balance of coverage, cost, and long-term stability.

---

## Step 8 — Explain Why the Recommendation Was Selected

Tie the recommendation directly to the client’s priorities and risks.

Potential rationale:

- Better catastrophic protection
- Improved business-income structure
- Better carrier fit
- Stronger contract compliance
- Better claims support
- Reduced coverage gaps
- More predictable cost
- Improved deductible structure
- Better long-term stability
- Broader wording
- Better cyber protection
- Better umbrella structure

Use 3–5 strongest reasons.

---

## Step 9 — Explain the Cost / Coverage Trade-Off

Every meaningful proposal has trade-offs.

Examples:

- Higher premium for broader protection
- Higher deductible for lower premium
- Lower limits for cost relief
- More retained risk for greater control
- Broader coverage at a higher cost
- Better carrier quality at a modest premium difference
- Increased complexity for improved long-term economics

Explain trade-offs directly.

Do not hide disadvantages.

---

# Trade-Off Transparency Standard

For every material recommendation, ask:

1. What does the client gain?
2. What does the client give up?
3. What risk remains?
4. What assumption supports the recommendation?

A trusted advisor explains both sides.

---

## Step 10 — Translate Technical Terms

When technical terminology is necessary, explain it in plain language.

Example:

Instead of:

> The umbrella follows form over the underlying GL.

Prefer:

> The umbrella is designed to extend the liability protection provided by the underlying general liability policy, subject to its own terms and conditions.

Instead of:

> The proposal includes a $100,000 SIR.

Prefer:

> The company would be responsible for the first $100,000 of a covered claim before the policy begins paying.

Do not oversimplify in a way that creates inaccuracy.

---

## Step 11 — Identify Expected Outcomes

Explain what the recommended program is intended to achieve.

Potential outcomes:

- Better protection against catastrophic loss
- Greater coverage certainty
- Improved contract compliance
- Reduced uninsured exposure
- Better premium stability
- Improved claims support
- More appropriate risk retention
- Better alignment with current operations
- Greater long-term flexibility
- Improved carrier relationship
- Better renewal positioning

Do not guarantee outcomes that depend on future claims or carrier behavior.

Use:

> Designed to...

> Intended to...

> Expected to improve...

rather than absolute guarantees.

---

## Step 12 — Identify Remaining Risks

A strong proposal narrative should acknowledge material issues that remain.

Examples:

- High deductible
- Exclusion that cannot be removed
- Limited cyber sublimit
- Catastrophe deductible
- Limited umbrella capacity
- Uninsured operational risk
- Client-retained risk
- Coverage requiring further review

Do not create the impression that any insurance program eliminates all risk.

---

## Step 13 — Identify Decisions Required From the Client

Clearly state what the client needs to decide.

Examples:

- Select Option A or B
- Approve higher deductible
- Approve umbrella increase
- Accept coverage enhancement
- Confirm risk-retention level
- Provide missing information
- Approve binding
- Select financing arrangement

Do not leave the proposal without a decision path.

---

## Step 14 — Establish the Next Step

End with a specific next action.

Examples:

- Confirm selected option
- Review one unresolved endorsement
- Schedule final proposal meeting
- Obtain client authorization
- Complete outstanding underwriting requirements
- Bind coverage
- Confirm payment arrangement

The proposal should move toward a decision.

---

# Executive Communication Standard

The audience may include:

- Business owners
- CEOs
- CFOs
- COOs
- Controllers
- Board members
- Senior executives

Write for a reader who:

- Is intelligent
- Is busy
- May not be an insurance expert
- Wants to understand the business consequence
- Wants to know what is recommended
- Wants to know why

Do not write down to the client.

Simplify the insurance language without oversimplifying the business decision.

---

# Decision Rules

## Recommendation Before Specification

Do not lead with:

> The proposed GL limit is $1 million / $2 million.

Lead with:

> The recommended program preserves the client’s core liability structure while increasing catastrophic protection through a larger umbrella limit.

Then provide technical detail where useful.

---

## Outcomes Before Features

Do not simply say:

> Includes Equipment Breakdown.

Prefer:

> The proposed property program adds protection for certain mechanical and electrical equipment failures that may not be addressed by standard property coverage alone.

---

## Explain Why

Every major recommendation should answer:

> Why does this make sense for this client?

---

## Do Not Hide Trade-Offs

If the recommended option costs more, explain why.

If the deductible increases, explain the retained risk.

If coverage is narrower, disclose it.

---

## Materiality Over Exhaustiveness

Do not summarize every endorsement.

Prioritize what should influence the executive’s decision.

---

## Plain Language Without Losing Accuracy

Simplify technical language, but do not materially change its meaning.

---

## No Unverified Coverage Statements

If an issue depends on policy wording that has not been reviewed, say so.

---

# Materiality / Prioritization Framework

Prioritize proposal issues based on:

| Factor | Question |
|---|---|
| Financial Impact | Could this materially affect the client’s cost? |
| Coverage Impact | Could this materially change protection? |
| Severity | Could the issue create a major loss? |
| Operational Impact | Could it interrupt the business? |
| Contractual Impact | Could it affect customer or lender requirements? |
| Decision Relevance | Does the executive need this to choose? |
| Client Priority | Does it align with stated goals? |
| Confidence | How strongly is the conclusion supported? |

Do not include minor technical differences unless they affect the decision.

# Failure Conditions

The Skill should not be considered complete if:

- The output simply restates quote details
- No recommendation is made when sufficient information exists
- The recommendation is based only on lowest premium
- Technical terminology dominates the narrative
- Material trade-offs are hidden
- Client priorities are ignored
- Material risks are not connected to the recommendation
- Unsupported coverage conclusions are made
- Unresolved issues are hidden
- The client cannot tell what decision is being recommended
- No clear next step exists
- The narrative sounds like a carrier quote rather than an advisor recommendation

If any failure condition exists, revise before finalizing.

# Guardrails

## Do Not Fabricate Proposal Information

Never invent:

- Premium
- Limits
- Deductibles
- Coverage
- Carrier
- Endorsements
- Exclusions
- Sublimits
- Claims
- Client priorities
- Quote terms
- Binding requirements

---

## Do Not Guarantee Coverage

Never state:

- A claim will be covered
- A policy definitely responds
- A limit is definitely sufficient
- An exclusion definitely does not apply

unless appropriately supported by authoritative information and professional review.

---

## Do Not Misrepresent Carrier Terms

Do not describe proposed coverage more broadly than the carrier documentation supports.

If wording remains unresolved, state that it requires confirmation.

---

## Do Not Hide Material Differences

If the recommended program:

- Costs more
- Has a higher deductible
- Has a lower limit
- Has a restrictive exclusion
- Has a significant sublimit
- Requires greater retained risk

disclose the material trade-off.

---

## Do Not Treat Lowest Premium as Best

The recommendation should consider:

- Coverage
- Limits
- Carrier
- Claims capability
- Risk retained
- Stability
- Client priorities
- Long-term fit

---

## No Legal Advice

Legal or contractual issues may be identified.

Do not provide unsupported legal conclusions.

---

## Protect Confidential Information

Do not unnecessarily expose:

- Personally identifiable information
- Claimant details
- Financial account information
- Confidential client information
- Proprietary agency information
- Sensitive employee information

Use only what is appropriate for the proposal audience.

Carrier B

---

## Example Output Excerpt

### Executive Summary

Western Industrial is facing a meaningful renewal increase regardless of carrier, so the decision is not simply whether to pay more. The more important question is whether the additional premium produces a stronger program.

We recommend Option B with Carrier B.

Although the annual premium is approximately $17,000 higher than the incumbent renewal, the proposed structure increases umbrella protection from $2 million to $5 million, improves hired and non-owned auto protection, and broadens equipment-breakdown coverage.

Given the company's commercial fleet and contractual exposure, we believe the additional protection provides a stronger overall balance of cost and risk.

---

### Key Risks Identified

| Risk | Why It Matters | Current Concern |
|---|---|---|
| Catastrophic auto liability | A severe vehicle accident can exceed primary auto limits quickly | Current umbrella is limited to $2 million |
| Contractual liability | Large customer agreements may require higher liability limits | Current limits may provide limited flexibility |
| Equipment failure | Specialized equipment can create both repair cost and operational interruption | Current protection appears narrower |

---

### Cost and Risk Trade-Off

Option B increases annual premium by approximately $17,000 compared with the incumbent renewal and increases the property deductible from $10,000 to $25,000.

In return, the client receives an additional $3 million of umbrella protection and broader protection in two areas directly connected to its operations.

The primary trade-off is greater retained property risk in exchange for stronger catastrophic liability protection.

---

### Expected Outcome

The recommended program is designed to provide Western Industrial with:

- Greater protection from high-severity liability claims
- Better alignment with its commercial fleet exposure
- More flexibility when satisfying customer insurance requirements
- Broader protection for equipment-related losses
- A more balanced relationship between premium and retained risk
